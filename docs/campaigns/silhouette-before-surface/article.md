# Silhouette Before Surface: Four Tests for Creatures That Behave

By Cody Haring / Ultramonkeydog Studios

A monster can look expensive and still say nothing. Texture can hide an uncertain body. A dramatic background can lend it a mood it cannot carry alone. A paragraph of lore can explain a threat the player cannot read. Strip those supports away and the useful question appears: what does this creature ask someone to do?

This is a practical design exercise, not a claim that every creature in our unfinished games already passes it. Work through four tests: shadow, action, consequence, surface. The order makes attractive detail answer to a stronger idea. It does not make beauty, strangeness or tenderness optional. It gives them something to belong to.

## 1. Shadow: preserve the information that matters

Flatten the creature to one value against a plain background. Keep the actual facing direction. Review it at the size it will occupy during play, beside the other bodies competing for attention. A silhouette that is clear on a full-screen art board may become an unreadable knot when the camera pulls back.

Ask where its weight sits, what reaches beyond its mass, and which empty spaces survive reduction. A low, wide body can suggest a different problem from a narrow, forward-loaded one. But no shape has a universal meaning. The interpretation must fit the camera, movement, animation and visual language of the particular game.

Choose the information you need the player to recover. Species identity, facing, attack reach and vulnerability are different questions. Do not award the design a single vague readability pass. If a creature is identifiable but its dangerous end is ambiguous, record exactly that failure. Fix the missing distinction rather than increasing detail everywhere.

Exercise: show two silhouettes without names. Ask someone to point to the threatening end and predict how each might approach. Their answer is evidence about that image, not proof that they understand combat. If both bodies produce the same explanation when their roles should differ, the anatomy needs another pass.

## 2. Action: give the body a verb

Write one verb beside the silhouette. Pull. Brace. Circle. Pierce. Shelter. Split. That verb is a design commitment, not a decoration label. A hooking creature should present a different spatial problem from a rooted blocker. A support organism should have a legible relationship with what it protects, even when it is not attacking.

Then describe the commitment in plain language. What space does the creature control? What warning precedes its action? When can the player answer? What remains exposed afterward? This turns anatomy into an interaction rather than an excuse for a stat table. The drawing and the encounter can now criticize each other.

A long limb is not automatically readable reach. Its pose might hide the joint; an effect might obscure its wind-up; the camera might compress the extension. If the attack surprises players for the wrong reason, inspect the whole sequence. The answer may be timing or composition rather than a different monster.

Exercise: write a three-beat description: promise, action, recovery. For example, an imaginary shield creature plants its body, pushes a corridor closed, then leaves a flank open while it turns. This is an illustrative design sketch, not a feature announcement. If the body cannot support the three beats, change either the body or the behavior before polishing.

## 3. Consequence: evolve the decision, not only the outline

Evolution is tempting to solve by addition: another horn, more armor, a larger number. Those can be satisfying, but they do not necessarily change the relationship. Ask what the evolved creature lets the player do differently—and what that power asks in return. A transformation earns its place when it changes a choice.

Preserve enough lineage to make the change meaningful. A recurring jaw angle, asymmetrical eye, gait or defensive habit can connect early and evolved forms without repeating the entire skeleton. The player should be able to recognize a history, not merely two unrelated assets sharing a color palette.

Consequence does not always mean punishment. A bonded creature might alter your rhythm by protecting a recovery window. An acquired trait might invite a different approach. A powerful body might occupy space in a way that changes positioning. The point is a changed relationship between capability, commitment and response—not an obligatory penalty attached to every reward.

Exercise: place the early and evolved designs beside one another. Write two sentences: “Before, I chose this because…” and “Now, I must consider…” If the second sentence is only “I deal more damage,” decide whether that is enough for this particular transformation. Sometimes it is. Do not claim a new identity when the intended reward is straightforward numerical growth.

## 4. Surface: make detail serve the read

Restore material, color and ornament one layer at a time. Each layer should reinforce a distinction or contribute an intentional emotional contradiction. A beautiful shell can cover a dangerous organism. A frightening body can protect something small. Clarity does not require making every creature emotionally obvious.

What matters is separating mystery from confusion. A player can wonder why a creature behaves tenderly while still understanding where its next attack lands. The unknown belongs in motive, history or consequence when the immediate action needs to be readable. A mystery is worth preserving; a hidden warning is not automatically one.

Exercise: compare the detailed version with the flat version at play size. Does the dangerous structure still lead the eye? Do ornaments compete with the face, joints or attack cue? Does the palette help distinguish roles without becoming the only way to distinguish them? Remove a beautiful detail if it consistently erases necessary information.

## Measurement is a witness, not a taste engine

Bounds, occupied area and broad shape comparisons can flag accidental sameness or wasted canvas. They cannot tell you whether an imperfection carries the design's soul. Two similar outlines may be correct for a lineage. Two different outlines may still feel interchangeable. A numerical difference is not a creative verdict.

Treat every automatic flag as a question with a named human reader. Inspect the original composition, the intended role and the actual camera before accepting or rejecting it. A review instrument becomes harmful when its convenient output replaces the question it was supposed to help answer.

This matters especially in AI-assisted production. Generating a new variation is not the same as resolving a design problem. Keep a specific reason for accepting it: a stronger facing read, a clearer action, a recognizable lineage, or an emotional contradiction worth keeping. “More polished” is too vague to guide the next decision.

The four tests are deliberately reusable: identify the information, name the verb, explain the changed choice, and restore the surface. Then return to real play. A still-image review cannot establish animation, fair timing, device readability or player understanding. The creature is finished only when the work answers the questions its game actually asks.
